## Abstract

The academic study of religion depends upon a technical vocabulary that allows scholars to compare traditions without assuming that every religion has the same concept of God, the same metaphysics, or the same understanding of salvation. Terms such as theism, monotheism, polytheism, monism, pantheism, panentheism, animism, syncretism, secularism, and religious pluralism are therefore not merely dictionary labels. They are analytical tools with distinct histories, disciplinary uses, and limitations. This article develops a carefully defined glossary for the study of world religions, beginning with the core terms commonly taught in introductory courses and expanding them with categories used in contemporary Religious Studies, philosophy of religion, theology of religions, anthropology, and sociology. It also corrects several common simplifications: atheism is not simply identical with nontheism; deism is historically more complex than the idea of an “absentee God”; dualism is broader than a conflict between good and evil; animism is no longer treated responsibly as a primitive stage of religion; and secularism must be distinguished from secularization and secularity. The purpose is not to force traditions into rigid boxes but to provide a disciplined vocabulary for description, comparison, and theological evaluation.

**Keywords:** world religions; Religious Studies; philosophy of religion; theism; atheism; agnosticism; monotheism; polytheism; henotheism; monolatry; monism; dualism; pantheism; panentheism; animism; syncretism; secularism; pluralism.

## 1. Why the Vocabulary of World Religions Matters

World religions cannot be compared responsibly by asking only, “What does this religion believe about God?” Some religious traditions are organized around a personal creator deity; others are not. Some speak of multiple gods, some of one God, some of an impersonal or transpersonal ultimate, and some resist the assumption that “God” is the correct comparative category at all. Religious Studies therefore uses a family of analytical terms to describe different claims about divinity, reality, religious practice, and relations among traditions.[1]

These categories are useful only when they are treated as **tools rather than verdicts**. A label such as “polytheism,” “monism,” or “animism” can summarize one dimension of a tradition, but it cannot replace careful historical and textual study. The same religion can contain multiple theological models, and the same term can be defined differently in philosophy, anthropology, history, or theology. Schellenberg, for example, uses *theism* broadly for the claim that at least one god exists, while noting that contemporary philosophers often use the word more narrowly for belief in a personal omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good deity.[2]

### Five Rules for Using Religious Categories Responsibly

- Always define the term before applying it. “Theism,” “dualism,” and “pluralism” have multiple scholarly uses.
- Distinguish a religion’s self-description from an outsider’s analytical classification.
- Do not assume a category describes every branch or historical period of a religion.
- Do not confuse similarity with equivalence: two traditions can share a category while understanding it differently.
- Use narrower terms when possible. “Cosmological dualism” is more precise than simply saying “dualism”; “religious pluralism” is more precise than “pluralism.”

## 2. God, Divinity, and the Question of Existence

### 1. Theism
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / comparative theology [3]

In its broadest comparative sense, theism is the claim that at least one god or divine being exists. In much contemporary analytic philosophy of religion, however, “theism” often functions more narrowly for belief in a personal supreme God possessing attributes such as omnipotence, omniscience, and perfect goodness.

**Scholarly caution:** Do not assume that every scholarly use of “theism” means exactly the same conception of God. A broad world-religions taxonomy and a technical philosophy-of-religion discussion may be using different definitions.

**Typical use:** Judaism, Christianity, Islam, many Hindu devotional traditions, and numerous traditional religions can all be called theistic in broad senses, but their divine ontologies are not thereby equivalent.

### 2. Classical Theism
**Academic use:** Philosophical theology / philosophy of religion [4]

Classical theism is a more specific model of God than generic theism. It typically understands God as the transcendent and ultimate source of all that exists and has historically been associated with strong doctrines of divine simplicity, immutability, impassibility, and necessary existence.

**Scholarly caution:** “Classical theism” is not a synonym for “Christianity.” It is a philosophical-theological model found in important Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions, while modern theologians within those religions sometimes revise or reject parts of the classical package.

**Typical use:** Augustine, Anselm, Aquinas, Maimonides, and major strands of Islamic philosophical theology are often discussed in relation to classical theism.

### 3. Atheism
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / nonreligion studies [5]

In philosophy of religion, atheism is commonly used for the proposition that God does not exist, or more broadly that there are no gods. In ordinary language and some social-scientific contexts, the word can also denote the psychological absence of belief in gods.

**Scholarly caution:** The word is polysemous. Lack of belief and positive denial are not always the same position. A scholar should specify which sense is intended.

**Typical use:** Philosophical atheism answers the metaphysical question “Does God exist?” negatively; sociological studies may use a broader category of people who lack theistic belief.

### 4. Agnosticism
**Academic use:** Epistemology / philosophy of religion [6]

Agnosticism concerns the epistemic status of claims about God or gods. It can denote suspension of judgment, the view that one does not know whether God exists, or a stronger claim that the question is unknowable.

**Scholarly caution:** Agnosticism is not simply a mild form of atheism. In philosophical usage it concerns knowledge or justified judgment rather than directly asserting God’s nonexistence.

**Typical use:** A person can be agnostic because evidence seems insufficient, because the concept of God is unclear, or because the person believes the matter cannot in principle be known.

### 5. Nontheism / Non-theism
**Academic use:** Comparative philosophy of religion / nonreligion studies [7]

Nontheism is an umbrella term for positions or religious orientations in which belief in a personal creator God is absent, noncentral, or rejected. It is especially useful in comparative religion because a tradition can be religious without being organized around theistic belief.

**Scholarly caution:** Nontheism should not automatically be translated as atheism. Some nontheistic traditions affirm gods, bodhisattvas, spirits, sacred realities, or forms of transcendence while lacking a creator-God doctrine.

**Typical use:** Buddhist and Jain philosophies are commonly discussed as nontheistic, although internal diversity makes blanket descriptions hazardous.

### 6. Deism
**Academic use:** History of philosophy / philosophy of religion [8]

In modern textbook usage, deism often means belief in a creator who establishes the world but does not subsequently intervene through miracles or special revelation. Historically, however, eighteenth-century deism was a diverse movement whose more characteristic concern was the sufficiency of reason or “natural religion” and skepticism toward claims of special revelation.

**Scholarly caution:** The popular image of a divine “clockmaker who walks away” captures only some deistic positions. It should not be treated as the complete historical definition.

**Typical use:** English, French, and American Enlightenment thinkers labeled “deists” differed sharply over providence, revelation, prayer, and the moral government of the world.

## 3. How Many Gods? Number, Supremacy, and Worship

### 7. Monotheism
**Academic use:** History of religions / philosophy of religion [9]

Monotheism is most commonly the belief that only one God exists. Philosophical accounts often add that this God is the unique ultimate divine reality and creative ground of everything else.

**Scholarly caution:** The term has a complex history and should not be projected simplistically onto every ancient text. Scholars debate when and how traditions such as ancient Israel should be described as monotheistic. The familiar evolutionary sequence “polytheism -> henotheism -> monotheism” is not a universal law of religious development.

**Typical use:** Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are conventionally classified as monotheistic, while their doctrines of divine unity differ significantly.

### 8. Polytheism
**Academic use:** History of religions / comparative religion [10]

Polytheism is belief in, recognition of, or religious orientation toward multiple gods. Polytheistic systems may include divine hierarchies, families, specialized functions, local deities, or a supreme deity among many.

**Scholarly caution:** Polytheism does not necessarily mean that all gods are regarded as equal, nor does it imply the absence of philosophical reflection on unity or ultimacy.

**Typical use:** Ancient Greek and Roman religions and many forms of traditional or popular religion are regularly classified as polytheistic. Hindu traditions may contain polytheistic practice while simultaneously articulating monotheistic, henotheistic, or monistic theologies.

### 9. Henotheism
**Academic use:** History of religions / Vedic studies [11]

Henotheism describes a religious orientation in which one deity receives supreme or privileged attention while the existence of other gods is acknowledged. It occupies a conceptual space between simple polytheism and exclusive monotheism.

**Scholarly caution:** Usage is inconsistent. Some scholars use henotheism almost interchangeably with monolatry; others distinguish belief or ranking (henotheism) from exclusive worship (monolatry).

**Typical use:** The term is especially common in discussions of Vedic religion and in comparisons where one deity is elevated without denying other gods.

### 10. Monolatry
**Academic use:** History of religions / biblical studies [12]

Monolatry literally concerns the worship of one deity. In a useful academic distinction, a monolatrous community worships one god exclusively while not necessarily denying that other gods exist.

**Scholarly caution:** Monolatry describes cultic allegiance more precisely than the metaphysical claim of monotheism. It should therefore not be used as a simple synonym for “belief in one God.”

**Typical use:** The category is often discussed in scholarship on ancient Israel and other ancient Near Eastern religious systems.

### 11. Kathenotheism
**Academic use:** History of religions / Vedic studies (historical technical term) [13]

Kathenotheism is a historical term associated with F. Max Müller for the successive elevation of different gods, each treated as supreme in the immediate context of a hymn or act of devotion. Müller later used “henotheism” as a shorter label.

**Scholarly caution:** The term is specialized and historically conditioned. It is useful for understanding the history of Vedic interpretation but is not a standard category needed for every introductory world-religions course.

**Typical use:** It is most relevant when studying the rhetorical exaltation of individual deities in Vedic hymns.

## 4. Ultimate Reality and the Structure of the World

### 12. Monism
**Academic use:** Metaphysics / philosophy of religion [14]

Monism is the view that reality is one in some fundamental respect. Philosophers distinguish several monisms according to what is claimed to be one: one basic substance, one fundamental kind, one ultimate whole, or one basic reality.

**Scholarly caution:** Monism is not the same thing as pantheism. A metaphysical system can be monistic without calling the one reality “God,” and a religious theology can affirm divine unity without being metaphysically monistic.

**Typical use:** Advaita Vedanta is often discussed as a religious-philosophical form of nondual or monistic thought, while materialism can also be a form of monism if everything is ultimately physical.

### 13. Dualism
**Academic use:** Metaphysics / philosophy of mind / theology [15]

Dualism is the claim that a given domain contains two fundamental kinds, principles, or realities. Because the domain can vary, “dualism” is an umbrella term rather than one doctrine.

**Scholarly caution:** Defining dualism simply as “the conflict between good and evil” is too narrow. Ethical or cosmological dualism is only one form; mind-body dualism, substance dualism, and other forms are distinct.

**Typical use:** Zoroastrian or Manichaean discussions may involve cosmological or ethical dualism; Cartesian philosophy is associated with mind-body substance dualism; Dvaita Vedanta uses “dualism” in a different God-soul metaphysical context.

### 14. Pantheism
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / metaphysics [16]

Pantheism broadly identifies God with the cosmos or rejects a real ontological distinction between God and the universe. Contemporary philosophers emphasize that pantheism is a family of positions rather than one perfectly uniform doctrine.

**Scholarly caution:** Pantheism should not be defined merely as “God is in everything.” That wording can also describe ordinary divine immanence or panentheism. The key issue is the identity or non-distinction of God and all-inclusive reality.

**Typical use:** Spinoza is a classic Western reference point; some interpretations of Hindu, Stoic, mystical, or modern religious-naturalist thought have also been described as pantheistic, though such labels can be contested.

### 15. Panentheism
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / constructive theology [17]

Panentheism literally suggests “all in God.” It maintains a real relationship between God and the world in which the world exists “in” God while God exceeds the world and is not simply identical with it.

**Scholarly caution:** Panentheism is not merely a softer word for pantheism. Pantheism stresses God-world identity; panentheism preserves some distinction. However, different panentheistic models interpret the “in” relation very differently.

**Typical use:** Process theology, some modern Christian theologies, and interpretations of certain Hindu and other traditions have been described as panentheistic.

### 16. Transcendence
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / theology [18]

Divine transcendence refers to the way God or ultimate reality exceeds, is not reducible to, or is independent of the finite world. In religious philosophy, transcendence may be cosmological, epistemic, or ethical.

**Scholarly caution:** Transcendence does not necessarily mean spatial distance, as though God were merely located far away. It is primarily a claim about ontological or qualitative excess over the world.

**Typical use:** Classical Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theologies strongly emphasize divine transcendence, although each also develops doctrines of divine presence.

### 17. Immanence
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / theology [19]

Divine immanence refers to divine presence, activity, or manifestation within the world. It asks how the sacred or divine is present to finite reality rather than only beyond it.

**Scholarly caution:** Immanence is not identical with pantheism. Classical theists can affirm profound divine immanence while preserving the Creator-world distinction.

**Typical use:** Christian doctrines of providence and Spirit, Hindu accounts of divine indwelling, and many mystical traditions use different forms of immanence language.

### 18. Ultimacy / Ultimate Reality
**Academic use:** Comparative philosophy of religion / comparative theology [20]

Ultimacy is an umbrella category for what a tradition treats as finally, most comprehensively, or most decisively real, valuable, or purposive. “Ultimate reality” is useful when “God” would be too narrow a comparative term.

**Scholarly caution:** Ultimacy is itself a theoretical category and not every scholar accepts the same definition. It should not erase differences among God, Brahman, nirvana, emptiness, Dao, or other tradition-specific concepts.

**Typical use:** Comparative philosophers use ultimacy to ask what a tradition regards as ontologically, axiologically, or soteriologically final without assuming a personal deity in advance.

## 5. Religion, Culture, Spirits, and Social Change

### 19. Animism
**Academic use:** Anthropology of religion / Religious Studies [21]

Historically, animism was associated with E. B. Tylor’s theory that belief in spiritual beings represented an early or “primitive” form of religion. Contemporary scholarship has substantially revised this framework. “New animism” often studies relational worlds in which humans, animals, ancestors, landscapes, or other-than-human beings are treated as persons or agents within networks of reciprocal relationship.

**Scholarly caution:** Avoid defining animism simply as “primitive people believe spirits live in rocks and trees.” That formulation reproduces the evolutionary and colonial assumptions now widely criticized in anthropology and Religious Studies.

**Typical use:** Indigenous and traditional religions may be analyzed with animist categories, but scholars increasingly ask how communities understand personhood, agency, kinship, place, and reciprocity rather than merely counting “spirit beliefs.”

Older introductory textbooks often retain a simplified definition of animism because it is easy to remember. That definition can be retained as a first approximation only if students are also told that the category has a contested history and that contemporary anthropology has moved beyond the nineteenth-century scheme in which animism was treated as the lowest rung on an evolutionary ladder of religion.[22]

### 20. Syncretism
**Academic use:** Religious Studies / anthropology / missiology / theology [23]

Syncretism refers broadly to religious mixture: the combination, adaptation, translation, or fusion of elements associated with different religious or cultural traditions.

**Scholarly caution:** The word is contested because it has sometimes implied that religions begin as “pure” systems and become corrupted by mixture. Scholars therefore also use terms such as hybridity, creolization, transculturation, and religious mixing, but these are not exact synonyms.

**Typical use:** Studies of Afro-Atlantic religions, popular Christianity, Japanese religion, South Asian religious interaction, and globalized spiritual movements frequently discuss syncretism or hybridity.

Theological and academic uses must be distinguished. A Christian theologian may use *syncretism* negatively for a mixture judged incompatible with Christian revelation, while a historian of religion may use the same word descriptively for cultural and ritual blending without making a theological judgment.[24]

### 21. Secularism
**Academic use:** Religious Studies / political theory / sociology of religion [25]

Secularism refers to normative, ideological, political, or institutional projects that organize the relation of religion to public life without granting religious authority automatic control over the state. There are multiple secularisms rather than one universal model.

**Scholarly caution:** Secularism is not identical with atheism, and it is too narrow to define secularism merely as “forcing religion into private life.” Some secular systems emphasize state neutrality, institutional differentiation, religious freedom, or equal citizenship.

**Typical use:** French laïcité, Indian secularism, American church-state arrangements, and Turkish secularism illustrate different historical forms rather than one identical secular blueprint.

### Three secular terms that should not be confused

| **Term** | **Academic meaning** | **Key distinction** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The secular | A category describing domains, practices, identities, or forms of life understood as nonreligious or differently related to religion. | A descriptive category, not automatically an ideology. |
| Secularity | A condition or mode of social/personal life in which religious and nonreligious options are configured in particular ways. | Describes conditions or lived forms. |
| Secularization | A historical/social process involving changes in religious authority, participation, differentiation, or social influence. | A process, not a worldview. |

Modern scholarship therefore distinguishes **secularism** from **secularization**. Secularization refers to processes by which societies or institutions change in their religious character or authority; secularism is a normative or political stance concerning religion’s public status.[26]

## 6. How Religions Relate to Other Religions

The final group of terms belongs especially to the **theology of religions** and the philosophy of religious diversity. The common threefold typology - exclusivism, inclusivism, and pluralism - is useful, but scholars disagree over its exact definitions and over whether it should focus primarily on truth, salvation, justification, or religious practice.[27]

### 22. Religious Exclusivism
**Academic use:** Theology of religions / philosophy of religious diversity [28]

Religious exclusivism maintains, in a relevant disputed domain, that one religious perspective is uniquely true, decisively superior, or uniquely salvific in a way incompatible alternatives are not.

**Scholarly caution:** Exclusivism must be indexed to a question. A person may be exclusivist about a truth claim but not about the possibility that outsiders can be morally admirable, rational, or even saved.

**Typical use:** Christian claims about the uniqueness of Christ, Islamic claims about final revelation, or other traditions’ unique truth claims may be analyzed as forms of exclusivism depending on how they are stated.

### 23. Religious Inclusivism
**Academic use:** Theology of religions / philosophy of religious diversity [29]

Religious inclusivism affirms the primacy or fullest truth of one tradition while allowing that genuine truth, divine activity, spiritual value, or even salvation can be present beyond its visible boundaries.

**Scholarly caution:** Inclusivism is not simply “half pluralism.” It continues to privilege one tradition while interpreting other traditions in relation to that center.

**Typical use:** Christian inclusivist models differ over whether non-Christians may be saved by Christ without explicit Christian faith and how other religions function in that process.

### 24. Religious Pluralism
**Academic use:** Philosophy of religion / theology of religions [30]

Religious pluralism, in the strong philosophical-theological sense, maintains that more than one religious tradition can possess genuine truth and/or salvific efficacy without one tradition holding exclusive or comprehensive privilege.

**Scholarly caution:** Do not confuse religious pluralism with the sociological fact of religious diversity. A society may contain many religions while a person living in it remains an exclusivist. Pluralism is a normative or philosophical position; diversity is a fact.

**Typical use:** John Hick is a classic modern reference point for pluralist theology, though contemporary pluralisms take many forms.

## 8. Why These Distinctions Matter for Christian Apologetics

A Christian apologetics website benefits from academic precision because inaccurate labels create weak arguments. If Hinduism is described simply as “polytheism,” Advaita, Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita, and many devotional theologies disappear from view. If Buddhism is called “atheism” without qualification, the student may import Western naturalistic assumptions that do not fit Buddhist cosmologies. If panentheism is confused with pantheism, serious metaphysical differences are lost. If every non-Christian position is called “pluralism,” the difference between religious diversity and a philosophical theory of religious truth disappears.

The Christian evaluator should therefore proceed in three stages: **description -> classification -> theological evaluation**. Description asks what the tradition actually teaches. Classification asks which academic categories illuminate that teaching. Theological evaluation then asks how the claims relate to biblical revelation. Reversing this order often produces caricature rather than apologetics.

- Do not call the Trinity polytheism or tritheism; Christian monotheism makes a distinctive claim about one divine being and three persons.
- Do not call the Creator-creature distinction “dualism” without qualification; cosmological Creator-creature distinction is not the same as two coequal eternal principles.
- Do not call divine omnipresence “pantheism.” Classical Christianity affirms God’s presence while denying God-world identity.
- Do not describe Hindu religious life with one label. The same tradition can combine devotional theism, multiple divine forms, and sophisticated metaphysical accounts.
- Do not use “syncretism” as an insult when doing descriptive Religious Studies; reserve theological judgment for a clearly stated Christian evaluative section.

## 9. Frequently Asked Questions

### Is monotheism simply belief in one god?
That is the basic definition, but academic discussions often add questions about uniqueness, ultimacy, worship, and whether other divine beings are denied. Historical application can therefore be complicated.

### Are henotheism and monolatry the same?
They overlap and some scholars use them interchangeably. A useful distinction is that henotheism concerns the privileged status of one god among others, while monolatry focuses on exclusive worship of one god without necessarily denying others.

### Is pantheism the same as monism?
No. Monism is a general metaphysical claim about oneness; pantheism specifically concerns the identity of God and all-inclusive reality. Some monisms are atheistic or materialistic.

### Is panentheism the same as “God is everywhere”?
No. Ordinary theism can affirm divine omnipresence. Panentheism makes a stronger claim about the world’s being “in” God and about the God-world relation.

### Is Buddhism atheistic?
“Nontheistic” is often safer as an introductory academic term because many Buddhist traditions do not center a creator God while still containing rich cosmologies, deities, bodhisattvas, ritual worlds, and concepts of transcendence.

### Is secularism anti-religion?
Not necessarily. Some secularisms are militantly anti-religious, but others are political arrangements intended to protect neutrality, equal citizenship, institutional differentiation, or religious freedom.

### Does religious pluralism mean that many religions exist?
No. That is religious diversity. Religious pluralism is a philosophical or theological position about the truth or salvific significance of more than one tradition.

## 10. Conclusion

The vocabulary of world religions is not a collection of labels to memorize mechanically. It is a set of conceptual instruments for asking better questions. **Theism** asks whether and how deity is affirmed. **Monotheism, polytheism, henotheism, monolatry, and kathenotheism** examine number, status, and worship. **Monism, dualism, pantheism, panentheism, transcendence, immanence, and ultimacy** address the structure of reality and the God-world relation. **Animism, syncretism, and secularism** belong especially to anthropological, cultural, and social analysis. **Exclusivism, inclusivism, and pluralism** analyze responses to religious diversity.

The most important academic lesson is that no term should do more work than it can bear. A category may illuminate one dimension while obscuring another. Responsible scholarship therefore defines terms, identifies their disciplinary setting, acknowledges contested usage, and returns continually to the primary beliefs, practices, texts, and communities under study. For Christian scholarship, this intellectual discipline is not a retreat from conviction. It is what makes theological comparison more truthful, more credible, and more useful.
